Flanged sections
& continuous beams

A slab and the beam beneath it are cast as one piece of concrete. Once you accept that, a rectangular beam becomes a T-beam — and it gets dramatically stronger for free. This page is about the geometry that follows.

What to notice: the block gets deeper very slowly at first. A 700 mm wide flange only 100 mm thick swallows moments up to 476 kNm before the block even reaches the bottom of the flange. Past that point the section stops behaving like a wide rectangle, and the equations change. Past 577 kNm the concrete runs out entirely and you'd need compression steel.

Those three regimes are the whole map. The rest of this page fills them in: the section geometry that creates them, the two design routes, how wide the flange is actually allowed to be, and how all of it plays out along a continuous beam.

01

SECTION ONEWhere the T comes from

In a beam-and-slab floor the slab is cast monolithically with the beams. When the beam sags, the slab sitting on top of it is squashed along with the beam's own top fibres — so a strip of slab on each side genuinely acts as part of the beam. An interior beam picks up slab on both sides and becomes a T-section; an edge beam picks up slab on one side only and becomes an L-section.

The good news is that you don't need two sets of equations. T- and L-sections with their flanges in compression are designed identically — everything below applies to both, with the only difference being how much flange width you're allowed to count.

What to notice: click any labelled part. The steel As always sits at the bottom of the web, as far from the compression as it can get — that distance is the effective depth d. Note also the transverse bars running across the flange: the flange has to be stitched to the web, or it can't act with it.

02

SECTION TWOThe flange only helps half the time

Compression has to be at the top for the flange to be useful. In a sagging span it is, and you get the full T. Over a support the beam hogs: the top goes into tension, the slab cracks, and cracked concrete carries nothing. The flange is dead weight there.

So the same physical beam is designed as two different sections along its length — a T-section in the spans, and a plain rectangle bw × h over the supports. That single fact drives the reinforcement layout of every continuous beam in section 9.

What to notice: the cracks in the deflected beam at the top of the diagram flip from the soffit at midspan to the top surface at the support. Watch the design section on the right lose its flange entirely when you switch to hogging — and watch the steel jump from the bottom of the web to the top of the slab.

The rule to memorise

Sagging → slab in compression → design as T or L with breadth bf. Hogging → slab in tension and cracked → design as a rectangle of width bw and overall depth h.

03

SECTION THREETwo conditions, one question

The neutral axis of a T-beam can sit in the flange or down in the stem, depending purely on the proportions of the slab and the stem. For a singly reinforced flanged section that gives exactly two conditions to consider:

(a) The stress block lies within the flange — s ≤ hf

All the compressed concrete is flange. The section behaves like a rectangle of breadth bf.

(b) The stress block extends below the flange — s > hf

Part of the compression comes from the narrow web. The section has to be treated as two pieces.

Be careful with the wording: the code's rectangular stress block has depth s = 0.8x, so the block and the neutral axis are not at the same level. It's the block we test against hf, not x. Drag the sliders to see the two go out of step.

What to notice: widen the flange and the block gets shallower — you gain area sideways so you need less depth. Two consequences follow. First, condition (a) covers the great majority of real beams. Second, because the flange hands you such a large compressive area, a flanged section almost never needs compression steel, which is why we treat it as singly reinforced throughout.

Watch x/d, not just the flange

For concrete class 50/60 or lower, x may not exceed 0.45d in a singly reinforced section. Push the moment high enough with a narrow flange and the violet line drops past that limit — the concrete is exhausted and you'd have to add compression steel. Section 7 gives the proper check for a flanged section.

04

SECTION FOURCase (a): the block stays in the flange

This is the easy one, and it's the reason T-beams are pleasant to design. If all the compressed concrete is inside the flange, then the concrete below the block is doing nothing structural — it's only there to hold the bars and carry shear. Delete it in your head. What's left is an ordinary rectangular beam of breadth bf.

What to notice: only the shaded top strip changes shape. The steel doesn't move, d doesn't change, and the compression force doesn't change — so the two sections have identical bending resistance. Nothing has been approximated here; it is genuinely the same problem.

So you use the rectangular-section equations from the previous lecture unchanged, just with bf in place of b:

K = M / (bf · d² · fck)step 1
z = d [ 0.5 + √(0.25 − K/1.134) ] ≤ 0.95deq. 3.4
s = 2(d − z) ⟺ z = d − s/2step 3

The middle two deserve a moment. The lever arm z is the distance between the compression force Fcc (acting at the centroid of the block, i.e. s/2 down) and the tension force Fst in the steel. So z = d − s/2 is not a formula to memorise, it's just geometry — and rearranging it gives you the block depth you need for the flange test.

The lever-arm curve

That square root is easier to trust once you've seen its shape. It maps how hard you're working the concrete (K) onto how much lever arm you keep.

The curve only bends sharply as K approaches the limit K′ = 0.167, which corresponds exactly to x = 0.45d. Beyond that the cap on z at 0.95d also matters at the low end: very small K would give an unrealistically long lever arm, so the code truncates it.

WORKED EXAMPLE ABlock inside the flange

A T-beam has bf = 800 mm, hf = 150 mm, bw = 250 mm and d = 420 mm. Concrete is C30/37 (fck = 30 MPa), steel fyk = 500 MPa. Find the tension steel for a sagging design moment of 300 kNm.

What to notice: the flange test in step 3 happens after you've already computed K and z. You always start by assuming case (a) and let the arithmetic tell you whether that assumption held.

A shortcut worth knowing

Testing s ≤ hf is mathematically identical to testing M ≤ Mf, where Mf = 0.567 fck bf hf (d − hf/2) is the moment the flange alone can resist. Setting s = hf in the stress block gives a compression force 0.567 fck bf hf at a lever arm of d − hf/2 — which is exactly that expression. Either test is fine; use whichever you have to hand.

06

SECTION SIXThe gate: is Md bigger than Mf?

Before designing anything, check the design moment against the flange's own moment of resistance. If Md > Mf, the flange alone cannot do the job, the block must reach below it, and s > hf.

Mf = 0.567 fck · bf hf · (d − hf/2)flange moment resistance

What to notice: Mf scales linearly with both bf and hf, but thickening the flange also shortens the lever arm d − hf/2 — so width is the cheaper way to buy capacity. This is why wide, thin flanges are so effective and why case (a) is the common outcome.

When the gate says "below the flange", the code offers two routes:

  1. The exact method — solve for the true depth of the neutral axis. More work, less steel.
  2. The conservative condition x = 0.45d — assume the neutral axis sits at the deepest the code allows. One formula, slightly more steel, no algebra.

We'll build the conservative route first because its derivation teaches the mechanics, then do the exact one and compare.

Where equation 4.1 comes from

Now take moments. The trick is which point to take them about: choose the centroid of the flange, where Fc2 acts, and that force vanishes from the equation. Only two terms survive.

1
Take moments about Fc2

Fst is a distance d − hf/2 below that point; Fc1's centroid is s/2 − hf/2 below it, and pushes the other way.

M = Fst(d − hf/2) − Fc1(s/2 − hf/2)
2
Substitute the two forces
M = 0.87 fyk As (d − hf/2) − 0.2 fck bw d (s/2 − hf/2)
3
Put in s = 0.36d and factor the ½
s/2 − hf/2 = ½(0.36d − hf) so 0.2 fck bw d × ½(0.36d − hf) = 0.1 fck bw d (0.36d − hf)
4
Rearrange for the steel
As = [ M + 0.1 fck bw d (0.36d − hf) ] / [ 0.87 fyk (d − 0.5hf) ]eq. 4.1

What to notice: that 0.1 in the numerator is not a code factor — it is 0.2 divided by 2, and the 2 came from the ½ in step 3. Every constant in equation 4.1 is traceable to geometry.

Equation 4.1 is invalid when hf > 0.36d

Look at the term (0.36d − hf). If the flange is thicker than 0.36d it goes negative — which is the algebra telling you the block never reached below the flange in the first place. If hf > 0.36d you are not in case (b) at x = 0.45d, so don't use this formula. Drag the flange slider below to watch the sign flip.

What to notice: the boundary is hf = 0.36d, the depth of the stress block itself. A "thick flange, shallow beam" section is really just a wide rectangle — and should be designed as one.

Before you use 4.1: check the concrete isn't exhausted

Equation 4.1 assumes the section is singly reinforced. To confirm that, compare the design moment against the maximum moment the concrete can supply at x = 0.45d. This one is derived by taking moments about As, so both concrete forces contribute:

Mbal = Fc1·z1 + Fc2·z2
Mbal = 0.167 fck bw d² + 0.567 fck hf (bf − bw)(d − 0.5hf)eq. 4.2

The first term is where the familiar 0.167 comes from: Fc1z1 = 0.204 fckbwd × 0.82d = 0.167 fckbwd², since z1 = d − s/2 = d − 0.18d. It is the same K′ limit you met for rectangular beams, applied to the web. The second term is the bonus the overhangs add.

SECTION NINEThe exact method, and what conservatism costs

The conservative route forces x = 0.45d whether the section needs it or not. The exact route asks instead: what depth of neutral axis actually balances this moment? Take moments about the steel, with the flange overhangs contributing a known amount and the web contributing an amount that depends on x:

M = 0.567 fck hf(bf − bw)(d − hf/2) + 0.567 fck (0.8x) bw (d − 0.4x)

Only x is unknown, and it appears squared — so this is a quadratic. Solve it, take the smaller root, then get the steel straight from horizontal equilibrium:

0.87 fyk As = 0.567 fck [ hf(bf − bw) + 0.8x·bw ]

What to notice: at the example's 550 kNm the true neutral axis sits at 162 mm, or 0.387d — noticeably shallower than the assumed 0.45d. The exact method returns 3 479 mm² against 3 537 mm² from equation 4.1: the conservative route costs under 2% more steel. Push the moment towards Mbal and the two converge, because the assumption stops being an assumption.

Why the conservative answer is safe

Equation 4.1 takes moments while holding s fixed at 0.36d, so it does not enforce horizontal equilibrium — the resulting steel force doesn't match the assumed concrete force. That mismatch is deliberate and always in the safe direction: it overstates how deep the compression zone needs to be, which understates the lever arm, which asks for more steel than strictly necessary. You get a valid design with one line of arithmetic instead of a quadratic.

10

SECTION TENHow wide is the flange, really?

We've been using bf as if it were obvious. It isn't. Compression doesn't spread instantly across the slab — it diffuses out from the web through shear, so the parts of the slab far from the web are less stressed than the parts near it. Rather than model that, the code replaces the real varying stress with a uniform stress acting over a reduced effective width beff that carries the same total force.

beff = bw + Σ beff,i
beff,i = 0.2bi + 0.1l0 subject to beff,i ≤ 0.2l0 and beff,i ≤ bi

where 2bi is the clear distance between the webs of adjacent beams — so bi is your half-share of the slab on that side — and l0 is the distance between points of contraflexure, the length over which the moment keeps one sign.

Reading l0 off the span

l0 is not the span. Because a continuous beam changes the sign of its moment near each support, the effective width shrinks there. The code gives you these values directly:

What to notice: at an interior support l0 = 0.15(l1+l2) — a fraction of the span, so beff collapses there. That's fine: over a support the beam is hogging and you design it as a rectangle of width bw anyway, so the narrow flange never gets used.

Two conditions on the layout

These l0 values are only valid if the cantilever is shorter than half its adjacent span, and if the ratio of adjacent span lengths stays between 0.67 and 1.50. Outside that, the contraflexure points move enough that you need a real analysis.

Usually you never leave case (a)

For sagging moments the flange gives such a large compressive area that the stress block almost always falls inside the flange thickness. In practice that means most flanged beams get designed as an equivalent rectangle of breadth bf = beff, and case (b) is the exception you check for rather than the norm.

11

SECTION ELEVENStitching the flange to the web

None of this works unless the flange and web act as one. The compression that the flange carries has to be delivered into it from the web, and it crosses the web–flange junction as horizontal shear. Without steel across that plane the overhangs peel away and you're left with a rectangular beam of width bw.

What to notice: the arrows show force flowing sideways out of the web into the overhangs. Transverse reinforcement must be placed across the full width of the flange to carry it. In practice you rarely add bars for this — the top steel of the slab's own bending reinforcement usually runs in the right direction and is already adequate.

SECTION TWELVEFire sets a floor on your dimensions

Strength isn't the only thing sizing a flanged floor. Concrete cover insulates the steel from a fire, and thin members lose that protection quickly, so the code prescribes minimum dimensions for a required fire resistance period. Pick a period and the governing numbers light up.

14

SECTION FOURTEENContinuous beams: the same design, moved along the span

Designing a continuous beam uses exactly the procedures above — nothing new is added. What changes is that the moment varies along the length and changes sign, so you're really doing the design several times at different sections and then working out how to make one set of bars satisfy all of them.

The tool for that is the bending-moment envelope: the outer boundary of all the moment diagrams produced by every load arrangement you have to consider. Reinforcement is then arranged with reference to that envelope, following the rules for anchorage and curtailment.

What to notice: the top steel is heaviest over B, where the hogging peak is, and the bottom steel is heaviest in the longer 6.0 m span. Bars aren't run full length — each one starts and stops where the envelope says it is needed, plus an anchorage allowance. Also notice the top steel at A and C: the analysis may have called those simple supports, but monolithic construction never really is.

Allow for the fixity you pretended not to have

In monolithic construction, where the structural analysis assumed a simple support, you must still design for partial fixity of at least 25 per cent of the span moment at that support. That's the 2 H16 sitting over support A in the drawing above.

Shear along the same beam

The shear-force envelope is built the same way. Mark on it the shear resistance provided by the minimum links, and wherever the envelope pokes above that line you need designed shear reinforcement — which tells you directly how much of the beam's length needs closer link spacing.

What to notice: the shaded regions where the envelope exceeds the nominal-link line are exactly the regions with H10 links at 150–200 mm; the middle of each span, where the envelope dips inside, gets the wider H8 at 250 mm. Link spacing is not uniform because shear isn't.

The tension bars have to be long enough to count

The concrete shear resistance VRd,c depends on the longitudinal tension steel ratio at the section — dowel action and aggregate interlock both rely on those bars being present and anchored. So when you design links, cross-check the bending reinforcement drawing: the tension bars used to establish VRd,c must extend at least d + lbd beyond the section being considered. A bar that stops just past the section doesn't count.

SECTION FIFTEENFour things that bite in detailing

These are the practical items that turn a correct calculation into a buildable beam. None of them changes As; all of them can change your section.

Anchorage into an exterior column

At an exterior column the beam bars resisting the hogging design moment have to develop their full force, and there is no beam continuing past the column to develop it in. The anchorage bond length must therefore be accommodated within the column — usually as a bend down into the column, or a full-depth L-bar.

Practically: check the column is deep enough to contain lbd before you finalise the bar diameter. Using fewer, larger bars is what usually breaks this.

Partial fixity at "simple" supports

Structural analysis often idealises an end support as a pin. Cast monolithically, it isn't — the beam and column are continuous through the joint and hogging moment develops there. If you provide no top steel, that moment cracks the top of the beam.

The rule: design for at least 25% of the span moment as hogging at any support you assumed to be simple.

Slab steel passes over the beam steel

At a support, the slab's top reinforcement runs across the beam and must sit above the beam's top bars while still keeping its own cover. The beam's top steel therefore sits lower than you might have drawn it.

The consequence is on d: the effective depth of the beam at the support section is reduced by the diameter of the slab bars plus their cover. Decide this before you compute d at the support, not after.

Bars have to physically pass through the joint

Column bars run vertically through the beam–column junction while beam bars from two directions run horizontally through the same volume. All three sets have to miss each other, and each still needs its cover and its bend radius.

This is resolved on the drawing, not in the calculation: set out the bar layers at the junction and check clearances. Congestion here is the most common reason a design has to be revised on site.
