01Why this problem: overlaying two maps

A city's GIS stores its road network as one layer of line segments and its flood-plain boundary as another. To answer "which roads pass through the flood zone?", the system computes the overlay of the two layers. Every place a road crosses the flood boundary becomes a new vertex of the combined map, one that existed in neither layer alone.

Each layer by itself is simple, but a country's road layer can hold millions of segments. Map overlay is the original motivation for the algorithm in this lecture (Bentley and Ottmann, 1979).

The same primitive shows up everywhere. VLSI and PCB design rule checks ask whether any two wires on a layer touch. Robot motion planning asks whether a path crosses an obstacle's boundary. Graphics clips one polygon against another edge by edge. Air traffic control checks whether two projected trajectories cross. Graph drawing minimises edge crossings, and line-of-sight checks ask whether a sightline crosses a building outline.

Key idea

All of these want every intersection, and the number of crossings K is usually far below n². A good algorithm's cost should scale with K, not with n².

02What exactly is a segment?

Definition

The segment AB is the set of points P(t) = A + t(B − A) for t ∈ [0, 1].

t = 0 gives A, t = 1 gives B, and every t in between gives a point strictly between them. Restricting t to [0, 1] is what makes it a segment rather than the infinite line through A and B. That restriction is exactly what makes "do they cross?" a nontrivial question.

03The naïve test: solve for t and s

Every application asks the same question: does segment AB cross segment CD? The obvious approach:

  1. Write each segment parametrically: P(t) = A + t(B − A) and Q(s) = C + s(D − C).
  2. Set P(t) = Q(s). That's two linear equations (one for x, one for y) in two unknowns. Solve by inverting the 2×2 matrix.
  3. The segments cross exactly when the solution has both t ∈ [0, 1] and s ∈ [0, 1].
Cramer's rule

The system a₁₁t + a₁₂s = b₁, a₂₁t + a₂₂s = b₂ has a unique solution exactly when D = a₁₁a₂₂ − a₁₂a₂₁ ≠ 0. Then t = (b₁a₂₂ − a₁₂b₂)/D and s = (a₁₁b₂ − b₁a₂₁)/D. Replace the column of the variable you want with b, take that determinant, and divide by D.

Where the naïve approach breaks down

Step 2 divides by the determinant. In the example it was −18, but it's exactly zero whenever the segments are parallel. With floating-point coordinates, a near-parallel pair has a determinant hovering near zero, and rounding error can push it across zero and flip the yes/no answer. Worse, a sweep line usually doesn't want a crossing point at all. It wants a cheaper question: is this segment above or below that one right now? Solving a 2×2 system just to answer that is overkill.

What we actually need is one primitive that never divides (only multiplies and subtracts), returns an exact sign rather than a coordinate, and answers both "do they cross?" and "which is higher?". That's the orientation predicate.

04The orientation test

Orientation(p, q, r) = (qx − px)(ry − py) − (qy − py)(rx − px)

It returns a positive number when r is to the left of the ray p → q (a counter-clockwise turn), a negative number when r is to the right (clockwise), and zero when the three points are collinear. What it really measures is twice the signed area of triangle pqr.

Where does it come from? Form two vectors from p: u = q − p and v = r − p. The 2D cross product u × v = uxvy − uyvx is the determinant of those two vectors. Since u × v = |u||v| sin θ, its sign is the sign of sin θ, where θ is the angle you rotate u counter-clockwise to reach v.

Gotcha

One slide line says a positive value means "rotate clockwise from q to r". It's the other way round: positive means counter-clockwise, a left turn, as the first bullet on that same slide and the 12 > 0 example both show.

05Do two segments cross? Four orientation calls

Segments AB and CD properly cross if and only if C and D are on opposite sides of line AB, and A and B are on opposite sides of line CD. In orientation terms, Orient(A,B,C) and Orient(A,B,D) have opposite signs, and so do Orient(C,D,A) and Orient(C,D,B).

If one call returns 0, that point lies on the line through the other two, but not necessarily on the segment. Check with a bounding box: its x must lie between the segment's endpoint x's, and its y between their y's. If all four are 0, the segments are collinear, and they intersect only if they overlap.

06The problem, and brute force

Input: a set S of n line segments in the plane, with K intersecting pairs. There are three versions of increasing difficulty:

VersionQuestion and best known bound
DetectIs there any intersecting pair? Can be done in O(n log n), which is optimal.
CountHow many pairs intersect? Output the integer K.
ReportList every intersecting pair and its crossing point. Needs Ω(n log n + K), since you can't beat the time it takes to write the output. Today's sweep achieves O((n + K) log n).

Brute force tests every pair: (n−1) + (n−2) + … + 1 = n(n−1)/2 calls to the crossing test, always, even when K = 0. It's only optimal when K = Θ(n²), a dense arrangement.

For scale: 10 segments need 45 tests, 1,000 need about 5×10⁵, and 1,000,000 need about 5×10¹¹. A country's road layer has millions of segments, so brute force is simply not computable there.

07The plane sweep

Turn a static 2D problem into a 1D problem that evolves over time. Imagine a vertical line ℓ moving from x = −∞ to +∞. At any moment it crosses some subset of the segments. Order that subset top to bottom by the y-coordinate of the crossing. That order is the sweep-line status.

The status changes only at a few discrete event points. Between events nothing interesting happens, so the algorithm jumps from event to event. There are three kinds:

  • the start of a segment (known at the beginning, because we're given the segments),
  • the end of a segment (also known up front),
  • the intersection of two segments, which we don't know up front.

The core idea: we don't know where the intersections are initially, but we can discover them with the orientation test whenever two segments become neighbours in the status.

08Two data structures

Event queue Q

Future event points, ordered by x, then by y to break ties. Initialised with all 2n endpoints. Intersection points are inserted as they're discovered. Operations, each O(log n): extract the next minimum, insert a newly found crossing, and check membership so duplicates are skipped.

Status T

The segments currently crossing ℓ, kept in a balanced BST ordered by their y at the current x. Operations, each O(log n): insert, delete, swap two neighbours, and find the neighbour above or below. The comparator uses the orientation predicate, not stored y values, because the y's change as ℓ moves.

Q says where to move to next. T says which segments are next to each other on the sweep line. Here is T's comparator at work when a new segment starts.

09The adjacency lemma: why this can work

Lemma

Let segments si and sj cross at point p, with no third segment through p. Then there is a position of ℓ strictly left of p at which si and sj are adjacent in T.

Why: walk ℓ back from p. Just left of p, nothing can be strictly between the two segments, since they're about to meet. Further left, segments between them can only appear or disappear at an event. So the last such segment to leave, or the later of si and sj to start, is exactly where they become neighbours.

Consequence

If at every event we test each pair that has just become adjacent, every crossing p gets scheduled in Q before the sweep reaches it. Inserting a segment creates at most two new adjacent pairs (above and below it). Deleting one creates one (its former neighbours). That's O(1) new tests per event, and no intersection is missed.

10Three kinds of event

At an intersection, si was above sj before p and is below it after. So si is now next to sj's old lower neighbour b, and sj is next to si's old upper neighbour a. By the adjacency lemma, any future crossing involving either of them must first show up as one of these two new pairs, so those are exactly the ones to test.

When a left endpoint separates a and b, they're no longer adjacent. If a crossing of a and b is already queued to the right of ℓ, it stays valid, because they may still meet. No action is needed.

T is a balanced BST holding at most n segments, so each operation on it costs O(log n). Every event triggers a constant number of those operations and a constant number of O(1) orientation tests, so each event costs O(log n).

11The Bentley–Ottmann algorithm

Gotcha

The slide's condition reads "q right of p, or q below p on ℓ". "Below" is from de Berg's textbook, which sweeps top to bottom. With this lecture's left-to-right sweep and Q ordered by x then y, the safe way to say it is "q comes after p in Q's order". The handler for a bundle of segments takes the lowest and highest of U ∪ C after the reorder, so all new adjacencies get tested.

12Degeneracies

13Running time

Events: 2n endpoint events, seeded up front, plus K intersection events, one per crossing. That's O(n + K) events.

Cost per event: O(1) orientation tests plus a constant number of operations on the balanced BSTs. T holds at most n segments and Q at most 2n + K ≤ 2n + n² events, so each operation costs O(log n), because log(n²) = 2 log n.

O(n + K) events × O(log n) each = O((n + K) log n)

Space: as written, O(n + K). T never exceeds n segments, but Q can hold every future crossing discovered so far. A known refinement keeps only crossings of currently adjacent pairs in Q, which brings space down to O(n).

The log factor comes from the balanced BSTs. Removing it, to O(n log n + K), needs fundamentally different machinery.

14A restricted problem: wires between two lines

Constrain the geometry. Put every segment's left endpoint on the line x = 0 and its right endpoint on x = 1. Call segment i wire wi: it runs from height ai on the left to height bi on the right, so a wire is just the pair of numbers (ai, bi). No two wires share a height on either side.

The geometry becomes purely combinatorial: only the order of the a's and of the b's matters, not the exact coordinates. Two wires cross exactly when they start in one vertical order and finish in the opposite one:

wi and wj cross ⟺ (ai − aj) · (bi − bj) < 0

Now apply the usual trick: sort the wires by left position, so wire i sits at left position i (counting from the top). Let π(i) be the rank of wire i's right endpoint, also counting from the top. In short, π is indexed by left order and returns right rank. Then for i < j, the wires cross exactly when π(i) > π(j). That's an inversion of the permutation π.

15Counting faster: cumulative sums

Process the wires top to bottom. When wire i arrives with right rank v = π(i), its new crossings are the earlier wires with a larger rank. Exactly i values have been inserted so far, so that's i minus the number of inserted values that are ≤ v. In picture terms: does this wire slope upward, and if so, how many earlier wires does it cut across?

So we need a structure that records "value v was seen" and answers "how many seen values are ≤ v?". Keep an indicator array A, with A[k] = 1 when value k − 1 has been inserted (shifted by 1 to land on positions 1..n). The question is a prefix sum, S[k] = A[1] + … + A[k].

16The Fenwick tree (binary indexed tree)

A Fenwick tree is a small array that answers running-total questions fast. It supports update(pos, Δ), which adds Δ at position pos, and query(pos), which returns the total at positions ≤ pos. Both are O(log n). It collapses the recursive halving into one flat array indexed 1..n, where position i stores the sum of a range of length lowbit(i) = i & (−i), the value of i's lowest set bit.

The range rule

BIT[i] covers [i − lowbit(i) + 1, i]: the lowbit(i) entries ending at i. For example, BIT[6] covers [5, 6] and BIT[4] covers [1, 4].

Why i & (−i)? Two's complement negation flips every bit and adds 1. For 6 = 0110₂, −6 = 1010₂, and 0110 & 1010 = 0010₂ = 2. Everything below the lowest 1-bit cancels to 0, everything above it cancels too, and just that one bit survives. We index from 1 because 0 has no set bit, so it could never own a range.

Gotcha

One slide writes the range as [pos − lowbit(pos), pos]. That's off by one: pos = 2 would give [0, 2], three entries for a length-2 range. The table on that same slide uses the correct [pos − lowbit(pos) + 1, pos].

17Counting inversions with a Fenwick tree

By step i, exactly i values sit in the tree, and query(v + 1) counts how many are ≤ v. So i − query(v + 1) is precisely the number of earlier indices with a larger value: the new inversions where i is the later index. Summing over every i counts each inversion exactly once. The +1 shift maps values 0..n−1 onto the tree's positions 1..n.

n iterations × O(log n) each = O(n log n), down from Θ(n²) for the double loop
Why the lecture calls this dynamic programming

DP means breaking a problem into overlapping subproblems, solving each once and reusing the cached result. Here the subproblem always has the same shape: how many values ≤ v have been inserted so far? Each BIT[pos] caches the answer for one fixed range, and that cached value is reused by every later query whose range includes it. Any prefix sum is assembled from O(log n) of those cached pieces, never rebuilt from scratch. For example, query(4) is BIT[4] alone, and query(5) = BIT[5] + BIT[4] reuses that same piece. The DP table is the BIT array itself.

Takeaways

  1. A segment is A + t(B − A) with t ∈ [0, 1]. Solving for t and s works, but it divides by a determinant that vanishes for parallel segments.
  2. The orientation test is one sign, computed with only multiplication and subtraction. Four calls decide whether two segments cross, in O(1).
  3. Plane sweep turns a 2D problem into a 1D one that changes only at events: starts, ends and crossings.
  4. Adjacency lemma: two segments that cross must be neighbours in T just before they cross. So testing only new neighbours is enough.
  5. Q (events by x, then y) and T (status by y, compared with orientation) are both balanced BSTs. That's O(log n) per event, and O((n + K) log n) overall.
  6. When every segment spans two parallel lines, crossings are exactly the inversions of a permutation.
  7. A Fenwick tree caches range sums of length lowbit(i), so prefix queries and updates are both O(log n). That counts inversions in O(n log n).

How it connects to part 1

Both lectures order points along a vertical line. Closest pair split the plane once per level and examined a y-sorted strip around the line. Bentley–Ottmann moves the line and keeps the y-order alive in a BST. In both, a geometric fact (packing there, adjacency here) limits each step to a constant amount of work.

Check yourself

Orient(p, q, r) = −5. Which way do you turn going p → q → r?

Right: a clockwise turn. r lies to the right of the ray p → q.

Orient(A,B,C) and Orient(A,B,D) have opposite signs, but Orient(C,D,A) and Orient(C,D,B) are both positive. Do the segments cross?

No. Line AB separates C from D, but A and B are on the same side of line CD. The lines cross somewhere outside segment CD.

A segment's right endpoint is processed. How many new pairs are tested?

At most one: its former upper and lower neighbours, which have just become adjacent. If it was at the top or bottom of T, there's nothing to test.

Why can't Bentley–Ottmann miss a crossing that's discovered late?

By the adjacency lemma, the two segments are neighbours in T at some sweep position strictly left of the crossing. They're tested the moment they become neighbours, so the crossing is in Q before ℓ reaches it.

π = [2, 0, 3, 1]. How many wire crossings are there?

3 inversions: (0,1) since 2 > 0, (0,3) since 2 > 1, and (2,3) since 3 > 1. So 3 crossings.

Which BIT entries does query(6) read, and which does update(5) touch, for n = 8?

query(6) reads BIT[6], which covers [5, 6], then BIT[4], which covers [1, 4]. update(5) touches BIT[5], BIT[6] and BIT[8]: 5 → 5 + 1 = 6 → 6 + 2 = 8.
