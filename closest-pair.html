01Geometry as a toolbox

Computational geometry is the study of algorithms for problems stated in terms of basic geometric objects: points, line segments, polygons and polyhedra. You get a set of objects and ask questions about them. Do these lines intersect, and where? Given a fixed amount of material, how much of a certain shape can we make?

The main idea that runs through all of it: use what we know about geometry, things like the triangle inequality, Pythagoras or convexity, to throw away most of the search space before doing any work. Here are four famous problems, each with that idea at its core.

02The closest pair problem

The rest of the lecture is about one classic problem. Given a set of n points, find the distance, according to some metric, between the closest pair. It turns up everywhere: which two aircraft on a radar map are dangerously near each other, or which two DNA sequences in a database are most alike.

Definition

For a set P of n ≥ 2 points and a distance d, the closest pair is the pair p ≠ q in P with the smallest d(p, q). Unless stated otherwise, d is Euclidean: √((x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²).

"According to some metric" matters. The same points can have different closest pairs under different metrics, because each metric has its own shape of "equally far".

The metric doesn't even have to be geometric. For DNA, a natural distance is the Hamming distance: the number of positions where two equal-length sequences differ.

03Brute force: measure everything

The obvious algorithm compares every point with every other one and keeps the smallest distance. The first point is compared with n − 1 others, the second with n − 2 new ones (its pair with the first is already done), and the i-th with n − i.

(n−1) + (n−2) + (n−3) + … + 1 = n(n−1)/2 ⇒ O(n²)

04Divide: sort once, then split the plane

Divide and conquer has the familiar shape:

  1. Sort the points by x as preprocessing, breaking ties by y. This is a one-off cost of O(n lg n), using merge sort for example. We sort once and reuse the sorted list.
  2. Split the plane in half with a vertical line, so half the points are in PL and half in PR.
  3. Recursively solve each half. In the base case, when 2 or 3 points remain, compute the answer directly. For 2 points the answer is obviously the two points themselves.
  4. Check across the divide for a pair with one point in each half that is shorter than both halves' answers.
  5. Return the minimum of these values.

Step through it on 15 points. Each split is just "cut the x-sorted list at the middle index", which is why the presort pays off: no split ever has to sort again.

Why the base case is "2 or 3"

A single point has no pair, so the recursion must never produce one. Splitting n points into ⌊n/2⌋ and ⌈n/2⌉ and stopping as soon as n ≤ 3 guarantees that: any n ≥ 4 splits into halves of at least 2.

05Conquer across the divide: the strip

Recursion hands back d₁, the closest distance inside the left half, and d₂, the same for the right. Now we must check whether a shorter pair exists with one point on each side of the dividing line. We don't need to check all such pairs, though, only the ones near the line.

Definition

Let δ = min(d₁, d₂). The strip is the band of width 2δ centred on the dividing line: every point whose x is less than δ from the line.

Why can we ignore everything outside it? A point farther than δ from the line is more than δ horizontally from every point on the other side, so its distance to them is more than δ too. It can't beat what we already have. And a short pair entirely on one side would already have been found by the recursion, because those are exactly d₁ and d₂.

The merge returns the minimum over three things: the best pair on the left, the best pair on the right, and the best pair crossing the boundary within the strip.

06The worst case: everything in the strip

The strip usually holds only a few points. But nothing stops all of them from landing there. If we then compare every strip point with every other, the merge costs n² and we've gained nothing.

When does this happen? When the minimum distance on each side (which is mostly vertical spacing, in this layout) is larger than how far the points spread horizontally from the line. Remember, the width of the strip is set by δ, the minimum distance between points, not by how far points are from the line.

T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n²) = O(n²)all-pairs check in the strip
Gotcha

Divide and conquer isn't automatically fast. If the combine step is quadratic, the recurrence is dominated by the root, and you're back to brute force with extra overhead.

07Only check the next seven

Can we avoid checking all strip points pairwise in the worst case? Yes. Take the points in the strip and sort them by y. Then for each point, compute the distance to the next 7 points only. If any is less than δ, update δ to that value.

We only look forward, because any pair with an earlier point was already checked when we were standing on that earlier point. And a partner closer than δ must be less than δ above us. That confines it to a δ-tall, 2δ-wide box sitting on top of the current point.

08Why seven? A packing argument

Suppose the closest pair is actually pl ∈ PL and pr ∈ PR, with distance δ′ < δ. First fact: both must be less than δ from the dividing line, and less than δ from each other vertically. That's Pythagoras.

δ′ = √(x² + y²)  ⇒  δ′² ≥ x² ⇒ δ′ ≥ |x|  and  δ′² ≥ y² ⇒ δ′ ≥ |y|

So if δ′ < δ, both points lie within δ of the line horizontally and within δ of each other vertically. Both fall in a δ × 2δ rectangle centred on the dividing line. Now the real question: how many points can fit in that rectangle without contradicting the minimum distances we found on each side?

Look at the left δ × δ square on its own. Every point in it belongs to PL, and all left points are at least d₁ ≥ δ apart. Try to pack as many as you can.

So at most eight points can sit in the δ × 2δ rectangle. Here's what that construction did:

  • It built the worst case for the x-distance: the candidate partner is as far from the line as possible. That's one point just on the line and the other δ to its left or right, which gives the 2δ width.
  • Since the strip is ordered by y, we only look forward. Earlier points already checked us. So the box only needs to be δ tall, not 2δ.
  • The box can hold 8 points only if the four left points are mutually δ apart at the corners and the four right points are too.

The current point is one of those 8, so the partner is among the next 7 in y-order. Every point between them in the order is also in the box, since it's in the strip and its y lies in between.

Aha

The number 7 doesn't depend on n. A geometric fact (you can't pack points that are δ apart densely) turns a quadratic merge into a linear one: at most 7n distance checks per level.

Gotcha

If your intuition says 7 is wasteful, you're right. The square-packing argument is generous, and the lecture notes that a more careful argument brings it down to 5. The exact constant doesn't change the O(n) cost of the merge, so any correct constant works.

09A first solution, and what it costs

Put it together: recurse on each half, sort the strip by y, and check 7 next points for each. Call this version closest1. In the worst case the strip holds all n points, so the sort costs O(n lg n) at every merge.

T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n lg n) + 7nrecursion, sort by y, check 7 next points
     = 4T(n/4) + O(2·(n/2) lg(n/2)) + O(n lg n)
     = 4T(n/4) + O(2n lg n)
     = 8T(n/8) + O(3n lg n)
     ≈ 2k−1 T(n/2k−1) + O((k−1) n lg n)
     ⇒ T(n) = O(n lg² n)k = lg n levels, each paying up to n lg n

Splitting in half gives lg n levels. Assuming n = 2ᵏ, it takes k − 1 splits to reach the limiting case of 2 points, where T(n/2ᵏ⁻¹) = O(1). In other words, lg n sorts of n lg n each. The initial sort by x is O(n lg n) and doesn't change that.

10Can we do better? Merge, don't sort

Sorting by y at every level is the expensive part. Can we avoid it? Borrow the idea from merge sort: merging two sorted lists takes only O(n).

So let each recursive call return its points sorted by y along with its answer. In the base case, sort the 2 or 3 points by y directly (constant time) and return them. At every level above, the two halves hand back sorted sublists, and we merge them. Here's the mechanism on the lecture's example, where the numbers play the role of y-coordinates.

T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) + 7n ⇒ T(n) = O(n lg n)recursion, merge, check 7 next points

Add the initial sort by x, and the total is still O(n lg n). To see what that buys you, here are the three growth rates on one chart.

11Side by side: closest1 and closest2

The two algorithms are nearly identical. The whole improvement is moving the y-sort out of the merge: it goes into the base case (on 3 points or fewer) and is replaced by a merge of the returned lists.

Gotcha

closest1 only sorts the points that are in the strip. On random data the strip is usually tiny, so it often runs fast in practice. The O(n lg n) per level in its recurrence is the worst case from section 6, when every point crowds into the strip. closest2 is O(n lg n) no matter how the points are arranged.

12Takeaways

  1. Computational geometry turns up in design, optimisation and graphics. Linear programming, convex hulls, largest empty spheres and ray tracing are classic examples.
  2. Brute-force closest pair is n(n−1)/2 = O(n²) comparisons.
  3. Divide and conquer: presort by x once, split at the median, solve each half, then check across the divide.
  4. Only points within δ = min(d₁, d₂) of the line can form a better cross pair. That's the strip.
  5. The strip can hold every point, so the cross check must avoid all-pairs. Otherwise you get T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n²) = O(n²).
  6. Sort the strip by y and compare each point with the next 7. Packing proves at most 8 points fit in the δ × 2δ box.
  7. Re-sorting by y at every level gives O(n lg² n). Returning y-sorted lists and merging them gives O(n lg n).
  8. The real lesson: knowledge of Euclidean properties narrows down the solution space.

Decision guide

Check yourself

Why do we stop the recursion at 2 or 3 points, not 1?

One point has no pair, so its "closest distance" is undefined (∞). Splitting n ≥ 4 into ⌊n/2⌋, ⌈n/2⌉ always leaves at least 2 per half, so stopping at n ≤ 3 never creates a lonely point.

A point is 1.2δ to the left of the dividing line. Can it be part of a better cross pair?

No. Any point on the right is at least 1.2δ away horizontally, so its distance is at least 1.2δ > δ by the Pythagoras bound. That's why it sits outside the strip.

Why is the forward box δ tall and not 2δ?

We process the strip in y-order and only look ahead. A partner below the current point was already checked when we stood on that partner. So we only need the δ above.

What is T(n) if the strip is sorted from scratch at every level?

T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n lg n) = O(n lg² n). There are lg n levels, each paying up to n lg n for sorting.

In closest2, why merge all points into Y and not just the strip points?

The caller needs the whole y-sorted list of its subproblem to do its own merge. If you merged only strip points, the next level up would be missing points and would have to sort again.
