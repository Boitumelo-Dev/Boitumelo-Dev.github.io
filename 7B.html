Design of RC Short Columns

Part 1 ended with a verdict: short. Now we size the steel. A short column only has to survive its first-order axial load and moment — so design is a matter of finding how much reinforcement pushes the interaction curve out past your design point.

Size the steel in one move — design chart d′/h = 0.20, fyk = 500 MPa

Notice Every section collapses onto one chart once you divide N by bh·fck and M by bh²·fck. The curve passing through your yellow point tells you the steel index Ascfyk/bhfck — and from that, the bars. Push M up and watch bars appear; make the section deeper and they disappear.

Series colour code (as in Part 1): blue = compression, rust = tension / steel pulling, green = capacity / passes, magenta = fails, yellow = you are here.

1

Three kinds of short column

Categories of RC short columns

Short columns are sorted by how eccentric their loading is: (i) short braced axially loaded columns; (ii) short braced columns supporting an approximately symmetrical arrangement of beams; (iii) short columns resisting moments and axial forces.

Notice Beam reactions land at the column faces, not its centre. Equal beams on both sides cancel each other's moment — that's category (ii), nearly axial. Unbalance them (different spans or loads) and a net moment appears: now it's category (iii), the axial + bending problem of Sections 6–9.

2

Detailing rules

Design requirements of RC short columns

Before any strength calculation, a column must satisfy a set of geometric rules. Build a section below and every rule checks itself live.

Cross-section

h ≤ 4b. Meet the fire minimum (Part 1, Tables 7.2/7.3). Cover 25–35 mm by exposure (Chapter 3, Table 3.3). Cover to main bars ≤ 40 mm.

Longitudinal steel

≥ 4 bars (one per corner; also ≥ 4 in a tied circular column), bar Ø ≥ 12 mm. As,min = 0.1N/0.87fyk ≥ 0.002Ac,gross. As,max = 0.04Ac,gross (0.08 at laps).

Links (ties)

Ø ≥ ¼ × bar Ø and ≥ 6 mm. Spacing ≤ least of 20 × smallest bar Ø, least lateral dimension, 400 mm — × 0.6 near beams/slabs and at laps of bars > 14 mm. Every corner bar held. No bar > 150 mm from a restrained bar.

Section detailing checker

Notice The default reproduces the classwork column (500 × 500, 8H25, H10 @ 300). It passes almost everything — except the 150 mm rule: the mid-face bars sit about 198 mm (centre to centre, assuming 30 mm cover) from the restrained corner bars. Tick "extra links" to fix it. Magenta bars on the drawing are the culprits.

Link spacing along the column

Notice smax is governed by whichever of 20Ø, the least lateral dimension and 400 mm is smallest. Near the slab/beam at each end — over a length equal to the column's larger lateral dimension — and through laps of bars thicker than 14 mm, the spacing tightens to 0.6 smax. Those are the zones where bars try hardest to buckle or burst.

Splicing bars between storeys

(a) When the column above is smaller, the lower column's bars are cranked inwards so they fit within the column above, lapping with its bars.

(b) When both columns are the same size, it's the upper column's bars that are cranked, so they can lap inside the lower bars.

(c) Separate dowel bars can provide continuity between the two lengths of column instead of cranking the main bars.

Notice A bend in a compression bar pushes sideways at the kink — the links there have to resist that force. If the change of direction is 1 in 12 or gentler, it's small enough to ignore; steeper (like the 1-in-10 crank sketched in the slides) and the link spacing at the crank must be calculated.

3

Axially loaded: two shares

Design equation of axially loaded short columns

N = 0.567 fck Ac + 0.87 fyk As (7.6)

Ac is the net concrete area, Ac = Ac,gross − As, with Ac,gross = bh. The two factors are just design strengths: 0.567 = 0.85/1.5 (long-term factor αcc over γc) and 0.87 ≈ 1/1.15 (γs). The column's resistance is the concrete share plus the steel share — nothing more.

Notice Steel is only about 1–4% of the area but carries roughly a quarter to over half of the load, because its design stress (435 MPa) is about 30× concrete's (14.2 MPa at fck = 25). The thin grey sliver is the concrete "lost" to the bars — that's why Ac is net, not gross.

Gotcha

Use the net concrete area. Using bh instead of bh − As double-counts the space the bars occupy: for the classwork column it overstates N by about 56 kN.

4

Worked example 1

Design an axially loaded short column

Problem. A short braced column, 350 × 350 mm, carries NEd = 2500 kN axially. fck = 30 MPa, fyk = 500 MPa. Find the longitudinal steel and links.

  1. Rearrange eq. 7.6 with Ac = bh − As: N = 0.567fck(bh − As) + 0.87fykAs → As = (N − 0.567fckbh) / (0.87fyk − 0.567fck).
  2. Concrete alone: 0.567 × 30 × 350 × 350 = 2 083 725 N ≈ 2084 kN. Shortfall = 2 500 000 − 2 083 725 = 416 275 N.
  3. As = 416 275 / (435 − 17.01) = 996 mm².
  4. Limits: As,min = 0.1 × 2 500 000 / 435 = 575 mm² (≥ 0.002 × 122 500 = 245) ✓. As,max = 0.04 × 122 500 = 4900 mm² ✓.
  5. Provide 4H20 = 1257 mm² — one in each corner. Check: N = 0.567×30×(122 500 − 1257) + 435×1257 = 2609 kN ≥ 2500 kN ✓.
  6. Links: Ø ≥ max(20/4, 6) = 6 mm → use H8. Spacing ≤ min(20×20 = 400, 350, 400) = 350 mm → use H8 @ 300. Corner bars only, so every bar is restrained ✓.
5

Classwork

Axial load resistance of the 500 × 500 column

Question. A concentrically loaded short column, b = h = 500 mm, has 8H25 longitudinal bars and H10 ties at 300 mm. fck = 25 MPa, fyk = 500 MPa. Find the axial load resistance N.

Show the full solution
  1. As = 8 × π × 25²/4 = 3927 mm² (bar tables often list 3930).
  2. Ac = 500 × 500 − 3927 = 246 073 mm².
  3. Concrete: 0.567 × 25 × 246 073 = 3488.1 kN. Steel: 0.87 × 500 × 3927 = 1708.2 kN.
  4. N = 5196.3 kN. The slide's 5197.6 kN comes from using the tabulated As = 3930 mm² — both are right; the difference is rounding.

Detailing sanity check: steel = 1.57% (between 0.2% and 4%) ✓; As,min = 0.1N/435 = 1195 mm² ✓; H10 ≥ 25/4 = 6.25 mm ✓; 300 mm ≤ min(500, 500, 400) ✓. But with only a perimeter link, the mid-face bars are ≈ 198 mm from a corner bar (30 mm cover assumed) — more than 150 mm, so in a real design they'd need extra restraint. Try it in the checker in Section 2.

6

Axial load plus moment

Design equations · design charts

Once moment enters, three approaches can find the steel: (i) design charts, (ii) solving the basic design equations, or (iii) an approximate method. Charts suit rectangular or circular sections with symmetrical reinforcement. The basic equations (or approximate methods) are for unsymmetrical steel or non-rectangular sections.

6.1 The basic equations — from the stress block

N = Fcc + Fsc + Fs = 0.567fckbs + fscA′s + fsAs (7.7)
M = Fcc(h/2 − s/2) + Fsc(h/2 − d′) − Fs(d − h/2) (7.8)

s = 0.8x is the stress-block depth; A′s is the steel in the more highly compressed face, As in the other; fs is negative when tensile — that's why the last term has a minus sign: a tensile Fs adds to M. Moments are taken about the section centroid. Drag the neutral axis and watch every term.

The mechanism: one neutral axis → one (M, N) point

Notice Same section as Part 1's demos (300 × 400, 3H20 per face, d′ = 50). Small x: the steel at the bottom is in tension (rust) and helps the moment. Large x: everything is compressed and M shrinks to zero. Once x passes h, the stress block is capped at s = h. You can't solve 7.7 and 7.8 directly for steel — you'd iterate x — which is exactly why charts exist.

6.2 Design charts

For symmetrical reinforcement (A′s = As = Asc/2), equations 7.7 and 7.8 are solved once for a family of steel indices and plotted non-dimensionally: N/bhfck against M/bh²fck. Each curve is one value of Ascfyk/bhfck. Each chart is for one d′/h — Figure 7.2 is for d′/h = 0.20.

Figure 7.2 rebuilt — tap the chart to read it

Notice At M = 0 each curve starts at N/bhfck = 0.567 + 0.87 × index — the axial equation (7.6) in disguise (without the net-area deduction). The kink in each curve is its balance point. The dashed x/h lines show the neutral-axis depth: above x/h ≈ 0.49 (the balance depth 0.617d, with d = 0.8h) the section is compression-controlled.

Worked example 2

Uniaxial bending with a design chart

Problem. A short braced column, b = 300 mm, h = 400 mm, carries NEd = 1600 kN and MEd = 180 kNm about its major axis. fck = 30 MPa, fyk = 500 MPa. Use the d′/h = 0.20 chart and symmetrical steel.

  1. Non-dimensionalise: N/bhfck = 1 600 000 / (300 × 400 × 30) = 0.444.
  2. M/bh²fck = 180 × 10⁶ / (300 × 400² × 30) = 0.125.
  3. Read the chart (try it above with n = 0.444, m = 0.125): Ascfyk/bhfck ≈ 0.35 (exact solution 0.353).
  4. Asc = 0.353 × 300 × 400 × 30 / 500 = 2542 mm².
  5. Limits: As,min = max(0.1 × 1 600 000/435, 0.002 × 120 000) = max(368, 240) = 368 ✓; As,max = 4800 ✓.
  6. Provide 6H25 = 2945 mm², three in each of the two faces parallel to b (2.45%).
  7. Check by section analysis at N = 1600 kN with 6H25: MRd = 197.8 kNm ≥ 180 ✓. Neutral axis x = 276 mm > xbal = 197 mm → compression-controlled.

Notice With 30 mm cover, H10 links and H25 bars, the real d′ ≈ 52 mm (d′/h ≈ 0.13). Using the 0.20 chart assumes the bars sit further in — a shorter lever arm — so it errs on the safe side.

8

Biaxial bending

Moments about both axes

Corner columns and columns with unequal beams on both axes see Mz and My together. The load effectively sits at a point (ey, ez) off both axes. Two routes:

Separate checks allowed if

(ez/h) / (ey/b) ≤ 0.2  or  (ey/b) / (ez/h) ≤ 0.2, where ey = My/NEd and ez = Mz/NEd are the first-order eccentricities in the directions of b and h. In words: one eccentricity is so dominant that the column is essentially uniaxial.

(The slide prints the second condition as (ey/b)/(ez/b); it should mirror the first, with h under ez.)

Otherwise, design for a single increased moment about the dominant axis, where h′ and b′ are the effective depths:

(a) if Mz/h′ ≥ My/b′:  M′z = Mz + β (h′/b′) My
(b) if Mz/h′ < My/b′:  M′y = My + β (b′/h′) Mz

Effective depths taken as h′ = 0.8h, b′ = 0.8b (d′/h = 0.20, matching the chart).

Notice The shaded wedges hug the axes: only a load point that lies almost on an axis qualifies for separate checks. Off the wedges, the weaker moment is converted into an equivalent extra moment on the dominant axis — scaled by the depth ratio and by β, which shrinks as the column gets more heavily loaded.

Worked example 3

Biaxial bending

Problem. A short column, b = 300 mm, h = 400 mm (b′ = 240, h′ = 320 mm), carries NEd = 1000 kN, Mz = 100 kNm and My = 60 kNm. fck = 25 MPa, fyk = 500 MPa. Design the steel. (These are the calculator's defaults above.)

  1. Eccentricities: ez = 100×10⁶/1000×10³ = 100 mm; ey = 60 mm.
  2. Separate checks? (ez/h)/(ey/b) = 0.25/0.20 = 1.25 > 0.2 and (ey/b)/(ez/h) = 0.80 > 0.2 → no — genuinely biaxial.
  3. Dominant axis: Mz/h′ = 100/320 = 0.3125 ≥ My/b′ = 60/240 = 0.25 → case (a), enhance Mz.
  4. N/bhfck = 1 000 000/(300×400×25) = 0.333 → β = 1 − 0.333 = 0.667 (Table 7.4 interpolated between 0.7 and 0.6).
  5. M′z = 100 + 0.667 × (320/240) × 60 = 100 + 53.3 = 153.3 kNm.
  6. Chart: N/bhfck = 0.333, M/bh²fck = 153.3×10⁶/(300×400²×25) = 0.128 → Ascfyk/bhfck = 0.287.
  7. Asc = 0.287 × 300 × 400 × 25/500 = 1720 mm². As,min = max(230, 240) = 240 ✓.
  8. Provide 4H25 = 1963 mm², one per corner. Check at N = 1000 kN: MRd = 164.5 kNm ≥ 153.3 ✓.

✓

Decision guide

Confirm it's short (Part 1: λ < λlim).

Detailing first: h ≤ 4b, fire size, cover 25–35 (≤ 40 to main bars), ≥ 4 bars of Ø ≥ 12, links Ø ≥ ¼ bar & 6 mm at ≤ min(20Ø, least dim, 400), × 0.6 in end and lap zones, 150 mm rule.

Purely axial? As = (N − 0.567fckbh)/(0.87fyk − 0.567fck) from eq. 7.6.

Uniaxial moment, symmetric steel? Chart: N/bhfck, M/bh²fck → index → Asc = index × bhfck/fyk. Unsymmetrical or odd shape → equations 7.7/7.8.

Biaxial? If an eccentricity ratio ≤ 0.2 → separate checks. Else pick the dominant axis by M/h′ vs M/b′, enhance with β = 1 − N/bhfck (≥ 0.3), then use the chart.

Finally clamp to As,min = max(0.1N/0.87fyk, 0.002bh) and As,max = 0.04bh (0.08 at laps), choose bars, check.
