Greedy take the best step you can see — and hope the view was enough

A greedy algorithm never plans ahead. At every step it grabs whatever looks best right now and never looks back. Sometimes that lands you on the summit. Sometimes it strands you on a lonely little hill. This page is about learning to tell the two apart — by feel.

Start climbing ↓
01 The one idea

A locally optimal move, repeated

Greedy means: at each step, make the choice that looks best in your immediate surroundings — and hope those local wins add up to a global optimum. No backtracking. No second-guessing.

The cleanest way to feel this is to become a hill-climber. You're a red marker on a landscape. You can only peek a tiny bit left and right, and you step toward whichever side is higher. Release yourself and watch where you end up.

↖ On one hill, every start reaches the top. On two hills, drop the climber in the left basin — it climbs the small peak and gets stuck, blind to the taller summit across the valley.

That trapped climber is the whole danger of greedy in one picture. The local rule — "always step up" — is perfect on a single smooth hill, but on a bumpy landscape it commits to the nearest peak and can never cross the valley to the real summit. The peak it's stuck on has a name: a local optimum.

Same trap, other problems In the Travelling Salesman Problem, the greedy rule "drive to the nearest unvisited city" feels obviously smart — yet grabbing cheap early hops routinely forces long, expensive detours later. Locally sensible, globally not. The nearest city is just another small hill.

So greedy is fast, simple, and myopic. The entire rest of this page answers one question: how do you know, before you trust it, whether greedy will actually reach the true summit?

02 The safety check

Two properties make greedy safe

Greedy is guaranteed to find the optimum only when the problem has both of these. They're different ideas, and they work as a team.

🎯

Greedy choice property

You can reach a global optimum by making locally optimal choices — and once you've chosen, you never need to undo it. No backtracking. Each choice shrinks the task into a smaller version of the same problem.

🧩

Optimal substructure

An optimal solution to the whole problem contains optimal solutions to its sub-problems. So you're allowed to ignore the big picture: solve the small pieces well and they snap together into the overall best.

Put them together and you get the promise of greedy: repeatedly solve the smallest piece, and you'll solve the whole thing — without ever backtracking. The problem keeps the same shape as it shrinks (greedy choice), and handling each shrunken piece well is enough (optimal substructure). Watch the problem collapse:

The shrinking problem interactive

Serve the smallest, and a 5-person problem becomes a 4-person problem becomes… the same problem, smaller. You never revisit an earlier pick.

These two properties are not trivial to verify, and whether they hold is entirely problem-dependent. Next we meet a problem where they both hold — and then one where they don't.

03 Where greedy wins

Optimal service: shortest job first

A queue of people each need a service that takes some time. You choose the order. Minimise the total waiting time across everyone.

Here's the mechanic that decides everything: whoever you serve first makes everyone else in the queue wait for their whole service time. Serve someone early and their time is felt by many. Serve them late and it's felt by few.

Notice what the bars teach you: the earliest served person carries the biggest multiplier (the most people are stuck behind them). So you want your smallest times to sit where the multipliers are largest — at the front. Sorting shortest-first does exactly that. Every other order can be improved by swapping a big job earlier for a small one.

Why it's provably optimal — the exchange argument

Take any order and look at two neighbours, A (time 4) and B (time 3), with everything else held fixed. Compare serving A-first vs B-first. Everything after them is identical; only the front term changes:

A first 4+ (2×4)+ (2×3)+ t(C,D)
B first 3+ (2×4)+ (2×3)+ t(C,D)

The two totals are identical except for the leading number — and that number is exactly the service time of whoever went first. So the only thing your choice ever controls is which "excess wait" gets stuck on the front. Minimise it every time (serve the shorter person) and the total is minimised. Do this all the way down and you bottom out at the two-person case, where greedy is obviously right. Local choice → global optimum. ✓

Both properties, spotted Solving the 5-person case hands you a 4-person case of the same shape — that's optimal substructure. And you never revisit an earlier pick — that's the greedy choice property. Brute force would test n! orderings (already 120 for five people); greedy just sorts.
04 Where greedy breaks

Making change: the honest failure

Given unlimited coins of fixed denominations, make a target amount with the fewest coins. Greedy's rule: repeatedly grab the largest coin that still fits.

On everyday currency this works beautifully. But swap the coin set and the same rule confidently returns the wrong answer. Run it yourself.

The killer case is 40¢ from {5, 10, 20, 25}. Greedy snatches the shiny 25 first, then 10, then 5 — three coins. But the real minimum is just 20 + 20 — two coins. Grabbing the big coin early was a trap, exactly like climbing the nearest hill. And 51¢ from {2, 5, 10, 20, 50} is worse: greedy takes 50, leaves 1, and there's no coin small enough to finish — it strands itself, even though an answer exists.

Definition A coin set is canonical if greedy always returns the true minimum for it. Real currencies are usually designed to be canonical on purpose. Arbitrary sets are not — so check first, then trust greedy.

Which raises a sharp question. Checking whether a coin set is canonical turns out to be fast. But finding the minimum-coin answer for a bad set, in general, is not. That gap between "easy to check" and "hard to find" is one of the deepest ideas in computer science — and it's where we go next.

05 Why "check, then greedy"

Easy to verify, hard to find

To talk precisely about "hard," we need a model of computation and two famous clubs of problems: P and NP.

Picture two machines. A deterministic Turing machine follows one path — from each state, exactly one next step — until it accepts or rejects. A non-deterministic one can branch into many possibilities at once, exploring a whole tree in parallel; if any branch accepts, it accepts.

Two machines interactive

Both run for f(n) steps. The question is always: is f(n) polynomial (like n³, fine) or exponential (like 2ⁿ, hopeless)?

Deterministic — one path

Follows a single line of steps to one answer.

Non-deterministic — many paths

Branches everywhere, then only needs one accepting leaf.
P
solvable in poly-time by a DTM

The genuinely easy problems. A normal machine can find the answer quickly. Example here: deciding whether a coin set is canonical — doable in about O(n³).

NP
verifiable in poly-time by a DTM

Given a candidate answer, you can check it quickly — even if finding it seems hard. Equivalently: solvable in poly-time by a non-deterministic machine. Example: finding the minimum coins.

Here's the intuition to hold onto. A problem sits in NP when finding a solution is hard for an ordinary machine, but verifying a given one is easy. The non-deterministic machine's branching does the "finding" for free by trying everything at once — so all you additionally need is that the checking step runs in polynomial time. Hard to find, easy to check.

Verify vs solve interactive

Feel the asymmetry. Checking a proposed answer is instant. Finding the minimum is the part that explodes.

The payoff for change-making Deciding canonical is in P (cheap: O(n³)). Finding minimum coins is in NP (verifying is trivial; finding is hard as denominations grow). So the winning move is: verify your coin set is canonical — then unleash greedy, sidestepping the hard problem entirely.
06 Carry this with you

The whole lecture, distilled

  • Greedy makes local, myopic decisions with no thought for the future and no backtracking — which is exactly why it's fast.
  • It's only optimal when the problem has both the greedy choice property and optimal substructure. These are problem-specific and not trivial to check.
  • Optimal service has both, so shortest-job-first is provably optimal — one clean exchange argument proves it.
  • Making change generally doesn't, so greedy can be wrong (40¢ from {5,10,20,25}) or even get stuck — though it's still a fine heuristic.
  • The P/NP lens explains the fix: verifying is cheap, finding is hard — so check that a coin set is canonical, then run greedy.
  • When greedy's blindness isn't good enough and you must weigh the future, you reach for the next tool: dynamic programming.
