Introduction to RC Columns

A column is a member that's happiest when it is squeezed straight down its axis. Everything in this chapter is about what happens when it isn't — when the load slides off-centre, or when the column is tall enough to bow sideways.

1

What makes a column

Principle of reinforced concrete columns

Definition

A column is a structural member whose height is at least 3 times its section depth, loaded predominantly in compression parallel to its longitudinal axis.

That "3×" rule is a shape test. Stack cubes the size of the section on top of each other — if three or more fit up the height, you have a column. Fewer, and it's a stocky block that behaves differently.

The 3 × depth test

Height ÷ depth

Notice Each grey block is one section depth tall. The verdict flips the moment the third block fits — it's a ratio, so a 2 m tall, 1 m deep pier is not a column but a 2 m tall, 300 mm column is.

In a building, columns are the vertical spine of the load path: slabs pass load to beams, beams pass it to columns, and columns carry it storey by storey down to the foundations. So a ground-floor column carries every floor above it.

Load path — slab → beam → column → foundation

Notice The axial force (blue bar) grows by one floor's load at every level, so the bottom column is the most heavily loaded — that's why columns are often biggest at the base. The section icon shows that real columns may also have to resist moment about one or both axes.

Why columns are efficient

In compression, every square millimetre of the section works at the same stress. A beam in bending only works its extreme fibres hard while the middle loafs near the neutral axis. That's why, for the material used, columns are among the most efficient structural members — and why we lose efficiency as soon as bending creeps in.

2

Three ways to classify a column

Types of RC columns

The same column gets three labels depending on which question you ask: how is it reinforced across?, who resists the sideways load? and how will it fail?

2a. By transverse reinforcement

Laterally tied: longitudinal bars enclosed by separate lateral ties (links) spaced along the whole length. The ties hold the bars in position and stop them buckling outward. The most common type — and the one Part 2 designs.

Spiral: longitudinal bars wrapped by a closely spaced, continuously wound spiral (helix). The tight spiral also confines the concrete core, typically for circular columns.

Composite: reinforced longitudinally with a structural steel section, either encased in concrete or encasing it (e.g. a concrete-filled steel tube).

Notice In all three the longitudinal steel runs up the column; what changes is the transverse "belt" around it — separate ties, a continuous helix, or a rolled steel shape.

2b. By loading arrangement — braced or unbraced

Wind and other lateral loads have to go somewhere. In a braced frame, walls (shear walls, cores) or bracing take them. In an unbraced frame, the columns themselves must resist them by bending.

Notice The braced frame barely moves — the wall soaks up the push, so its columns stay nearly straight. The unbraced frame sways and its columns bend in double curvature (shaded moment). That sway is why unbraced columns get much longer effective lengths in Section 5.

2c. By failure mode — short or slender

A short column fails when the material reaches its ultimate load (crushing). A slender column fails by buckling — it bows sideways before the material is exhausted.

Notice Crushing capacity doesn't care about height (flat line); buckling capacity falls with 1/height² (Euler curve). The column fails by whichever is lower — the green envelope. Past the crossover, stronger concrete barely helps — buckling depends on stiffness EI and length, not strength. Illustrative only: Euler buckling with a reduced stiffness of 10 GPa; codes handle slenderness with λlim (Section 5) instead.

3

Where the load lands matters

Loading action on RC columns

A column can be (i) concentrically loaded — the axial load acts through the centroid; (ii) eccentrically loaded with small eccentricity; or (iii) eccentrically loaded with large eccentricity. Step through them and watch the strain across the section.

Concentric: uniform strain εc = εcmax everywhere; every bar and every bit of concrete is at its limit. The capacity Pro is simply concrete share + steel share, and failure comes after the longitudinal steel yields.

Small eccentricity: the strain is no longer uniform — the far face is less squeezed. Stresses there are below their maximum, so the section can carry less: P < Pro. The whole section is usually still in compression.

Large eccentricity: the far side goes into tension and its steel stretches well past yield (εs ≫ εy). The moment M = P·e dominates and the column behaves like a beam in flexure; P becomes small (→ 0 in pure bending).

Notice On the right, the capacity curve only ever goes down as e grows — the extent of the drop depends on how big e is. The extreme compression fibre is always at εcmax; what changes is how the rest of the section is strained.

Key relation

An axial load N at eccentricity e is statically identical to N acting at the centroid plus a moment M = N·e. That's why eccentric load and "axial + bending" are the same design problem.

4

The interaction curve

Use of interaction curves for preliminary design

Plot every (M, N) pair a section can just carry: bending moment on the horizontal axis, axial load on the vertical. The result is the interaction curve. Any design load inside it is safe. In general, the more compression a column carries, the less moment it can take — except for the bulge at the bottom, which is the large-eccentricity, beam-like part.

Check a design load against the envelope

Notice The dashed ray from the origin is the line of constant eccentricity e = M/N; where it hits the curve is the capacity at that e. Above the Nbal line, failure is compression-controlled; below, the tension steel yields first. Add bars or raise fck and the whole envelope inflates.

The balance point b

At point b: N = Nbal, M = Mbal, and the tension steel has just yielded, fs = −0.87fyk, at the same instant the concrete reaches εcmax. If NEd > Nbal the section fails in compression; if NEd < Nbal there is an initial tensile failure with yielding of As.

Walk along the curve

Notice Each point on the curve is one neutral-axis position. Drag x down from the top: the tension steel stress fs becomes negative and grows until it locks at −435 MPa (−0.87 × 500) — the instant that happens is point b. Below b the tension steel stays at yield, so as N drops the moment capacity falls too.

Gotcha

"Higher axial load = less moment" is only true above the balance point. Below it, a little compression actually helps: it delays the tension steel yielding, so a lightly loaded column can resist more moment than an unloaded one. Don't assume reducing N is always conservative.

5

Short or slender?

Slenderness ratio · effective length · limiting slenderness

The whole of Section 5 builds one comparison: the column's actual slenderness λ against a limit λlim. To get λ you need an effective length l0 and a radius of gyration i. Let's build each piece.

5.1 Slenderness ratio λ

λ = l0 / i = l0 / √(I/A) (7.1)

l0 is the effective height, I the second moment of area about the axis considered, A the cross-sectional area. The radius of gyration i = √(I/A) is the distance at which, if you squeezed all the area into thin strips, you'd get the same I. For a rectangle bent about an axis parallel to b, i = h/√12 ≈ 0.289h; for a circle, i = D/4.

Notice The dashed lines sit at ±i from each axis. A rectangle has two i values — the column will buckle about the axis with the smaller i, i.e. bending across its thin dimension, giving the larger λ. Always check both.

5.2 Effective length l0 — buckling modes

The effective height l0 is the height of an equivalent pin-ended column with the same section and the same buckling load. Stiffer end restraint shortens it; freedom to sway lengthens it. SANS 51992-1-1 illustrates these isolated-member cases:

Notice l0 is the distance between points of zero curvature (inflection points) on the buckled shape — the bit that looks like a pinned column. Fixing both ends halves it; letting the top sway freely (cantilever) doubles it.

5.3 Effective length from the frame — k1, k2

Real columns aren't perfectly pinned or fixed; the beams at each end give partial restraint. SANS / EC2 capture this with the relative flexibility k at each end:

k = column stiffness / Σ beam stiffness = (EI/l)col / Σ 2(EI/l)beam = (I/l)col / Σ 2(I/l)beam

k = 0 means a perfectly rigid (fixed) end; large k means the beams are flimsy and the end is nearly pinned. Then:

Braced: l0 = 0.5 l √[(1 + k1/(0.45 + k1))(1 + k2/(0.45 + k2))] (7.2)
Unbraced, the larger of: l0 = l √(1 + 10 k1k2/(k1+k2)) (7.3a)
         l0 = l (1 + k1/(1+k1))(1 + k2/(1+k2)) (7.3b)

where l is the clear height between end restraints.

Effective length calculator

Get k from member sizes (symmetric frame, two equal beams at each end)

For a typical column in a symmetrical frame with roughly equal spans, both beams at a joint contribute 2(I/l) each, so k1 = k2 = (I/l)col / [2 × 2(I/l)beam] = ¼ (I/l)col/(I/l)beam.

Notice Try k = 0 at both ends: braced gives 0.5l (fixed–fixed), unbraced gives 1.0l (sway with fixed ends) — matching modes d) and e) above. Then push k up: the braced value creeps toward 1.0l (pinned) while the unbraced value shoots past 2l. Sway is expensive.

5.4 Limiting slenderness λlim

Unbraced: λlim = 10.8 / √(NEd / Acfcd) (7.4)
Braced:  λlim = 26.2 / √(NEd / Acfcd) (7.5)

NEd is the design ultimate axial load, Ac the cross-sectional area, and fcd = 0.85fck/1.5 the design concrete strength. The group n = NEd/(Acfcd) is how hard the column is working. If λ < λlim → short; otherwise → slender, and second-order effects must be included in design.

Notice λlim falls as the load ratio n rises — a hard-working column is more sensitive to second-order (P-Δ) effects, so it's allowed less slenderness. And the braced limit is about 2.4× the unbraced one (26.2/10.8).

5.5 Worked example — classify a column

Problem. A 300 × 300 mm column, clear height l = 3.6 m, sits in a symmetrical frame with 300 × 600 mm beams spanning 6 m on both sides at top and bottom. NEd = 1500 kN, fck = 30 MPa. Is it short or slender (a) if braced, (b) if unbraced?

  1. Stiffnesses. Ibeam = 300×600³/12 = 5.40×10⁹ mm⁴ → (I/l)beam = 5.40×10⁹/6000 = 9.00×10⁵ mm³. Icol = 300⁴/12 = 6.75×10⁸ mm⁴ → (I/l)col = 6.75×10⁸/3600 = 1.875×10⁵ mm³.
  2. Relative flexibility: k1 = k2 = ¼ × 1.875×10⁵ / 9.00×10⁵ = 0.0521. Very stiff beams → nearly fixed ends.
  3. Braced effective length (7.2): l0 = 0.5 × 3600 × (1 + 0.0521/0.5021) = 0.5519 × 3600 = 1987 mm.
  4. Radius of gyration: i = 300/√12 = 86.6 mm (same both axes — square). λ = 1987/86.6 = 22.9.
  5. fcd = 0.85 × 30/1.5 = 17.0 MPa. n = 1 500 000 / (90 000 × 17.0) = 0.980.
  6. Braced limit (7.5): λlim = 26.2/√0.980 = 26.5. Since 22.9 < 26.5 → SHORT (braced).
  7. Unbraced: 7.3a gives √(1 + 10 × 0.0521²/0.1042) = √(1 + 5×0.0521) = 1.1227; 7.3b gives (1 + 0.0521/1.0521)² = 1.1015. Larger → l0 = 1.1227 × 3600 = 4042 mm, λ = 4042/86.6 = 46.7.
  8. Unbraced limit (7.4): λlim = 10.8/√0.980 = 10.9. Since 46.7 > 10.9 → SLENDER (unbraced) — second-order effects must be designed for.

Notice Same column, same load — bracing alone flips the verdict. Unbraced doubles l0 and cuts λlim by 2.4×. (All numbers re-run in code before publishing.)

6

Fire resistance

Minimum dimensions for a fire period (SANS 51992-1-1-1)

Concrete protects its steel in a fire by sheer bulk: a fatter column heats up more slowly in its core. So fire resistance sets a minimum column dimension for a required period (in hours), depending on aggregate type, protective plaster, and whether all faces or one face are exposed.

Notice Plaster buys a lot: unprotected siliceous concrete needs 450 mm for 4 h with all faces exposed, but with 15 mm vermiculite/gypsum plaster only 275 mm. With just one face exposed, the requirements drop sharply (300 mm instead of 450 mm for 4 h unprotected). Lightweight and limestone aggregates beat siliceous ones at long durations.

Table notes: vermiculite/gypsum plaster should have a mix ratio in the range 1.5:1 to 2:1 by volume. "Sprayed asbestos" appears because the table is historical; it is not used in new construction.

✓

Decision guide

Is it a column? Height ≥ 3 × section depth, mainly axial compression.

Braced or unbraced? Who resists lateral load — walls/bracing (braced) or column bending (unbraced)?

End restraint: k = (I/l)col / Σ2(I/l)beam at each end → l0 from 7.2 (braced) or the larger of 7.3a/7.3b (unbraced), or read Table 7.1.

Slenderness: λ = l0/i, with i = h/√12 for rectangles — check both axes.

Limit: n = NEd/(Acfcd), fcd = 0.85fck/1.5; λlim = 26.2/√n (braced) or 10.8/√n (unbraced).

λ < λlim → short (design for N and M with the interaction curve — Part 2). Otherwise slender: add second-order effects.

Fire: check the minimum dimension in Table 7.2 (all faces) or 7.3 (one face).

Eccentricity kills capacity

N at e ≡ N + M = N·e. The larger e, the lower the axial capacity.

Balance point

fs = −0.87fyk at Nbal. Above: compression failure. Below: tension yielding first.

Sway is expensive

Unbraced l0 ≥ l; braced l0 between 0.5l and l.
